Why Is Knowing History Important?

  • Baha Erdoğan
  • History

We are surrounded by more information than we know what to do with. A quick search will teach you, at least on the surface, how to change a tyre, what to cook for dinner or what is happening in world politics. But finding an answer quickly isn't the same as using knowledge well.

It's easy to confuse having access to information with actually knowing something. The difference is subtle but important. Real knowledge stays with you, and it shapes your decisions, often without you noticing.

When knowing in advance makes the difference

A simple example. One day you get a flat tyre and, with some struggle, change it yourself. Along the way you learn how a tyre is built and how much tread depth matters when you brake on a wet road. A few months later you need new tyres. You remember that experience and choose a model with deep tread instead of simply the cheapest one. You save money in the long run, and you drive more safely.

A lot of decisions work like that. What we need isn't always faster information. Often it's deeper knowledge, the kind that is already in your head before the moment you need it. That is where learning from the past, and especially from history, comes in.

History is more than a school subject

History isn't just names and dates we were made to memorise at school. It is a vast record of people, the choices they made and what came of them. Knowing what happened before helps us understand what is happening now.

Many of today's big questions have historical roots. Financial bubbles are a good example. Tulip mania in the Netherlands in the 1630s, the South Sea Bubble in 1720 and the dot-com crash of 2000 followed remarkably similar patterns, and each time many people were convinced that this time was different. If you know how similar situations played out before, you are in a much better position to understand them, respond to them and help change them.

It's not only facts, it's a way of thinking

What often gets overlooked is how engaging with history shapes the way we think. It isn't about memorising who did what and when. It teaches you to ask questions, to connect the dots, to weigh sources that contradict each other and to look at events from more than one side. You start to see how complicated people and their decisions really are.

It also teaches humility. You realise that people in the past had hopes, fears and hard choices just like we do, and that they usually had to decide with incomplete information. That perspective makes you more thoughtful in how you judge the world and the people in it.

Don't be the donkey

There is a Turkish saying: "Even a donkey doesn't fall into the same hole twice." If you don't learn from mistakes, you repeat them. The saying is a reminder that, as people who can read and reflect, we have even less excuse to fall into the holes others have already fallen into.

Taking the time to really learn history gives you a quiet kind of strength. You make better choices, you see the situation more clearly, and you keep the big picture in view while everyone around you is reacting to the moment.

Slow down and learn more

Yes, we live in a fast age. But nothing worth knowing comes in a hurry. Some of the most useful knowledge comes from looking back, so that we can move forward with more clarity and confidence.

History isn't only about the past. It's about understanding how we got here, and what we might do differently next time.
