The Ergenekon Epic

  • Baha Erdoğan
  • History

I should say first that I am not a Turkologist. I wrote this because I kept struggling to find a good, readable source in English when non-Turkish friends asked me about Ergenekon. What follows is the story as it is usually told in Turkey, with a short note at the end on where it comes from.

Defeat

For many years a bitter war raged between the Turks and the Tatars. The Turks, known for their courage and their skill in war, won battle after battle. As the old telling puts it, "there was no land in the Turkish realm where a Turkish arrow did not fly, where Turkish strength did not reach, and where the people did not bow to the Turks." The Turks were led by İl Han, the Tatars by Sevinç Han.

Sevinç Han managed to win the Kyrgyz and several other tribes over to his side. In a battle stained by treachery, the Turks were defeated. Almost all of them were killed and their line came close to disappearing. Only İl Han's youngest son, Kıyan, and his nephew Nüküz survived, taken captive along with their wives.

Into Ergenekon

One day the two of them managed to escape and met at the place where their people had once camped. The camels, horses, cattle and sheep that had also slipped away from the enemy gathered there with them. They knew that if they stayed, they would soon be found and killed. So they decided to take their herds to lands no one had ever set foot in, and leave no trace behind.

They followed the tracks of wild goats up into the mountains, crossed a ridge and passed through a narrow gorge. When they looked back, they saw that the path they had taken was so steep that even a sure-footed goat would struggle to cross it.

The land they reached was vast and untouched, without a single path. It was full of fruit, plants, game and running water. They gave thanks to God, settled there and named the place Ergenekon. This hidden refuge became the new home of the survivors.

Four hundred years

In Ergenekon the families of Kıyan and Nüküz grew. "Years passed and time flowed on. Kıyan had many children, Nüküz fewer. From Kıyan's line came the Kıyat, and from Nüküz's line the Nüküz and the Düzlügin." Over four hundred years they became so numerous that the valley could no longer hold them.

Wanting to break out of their confinement, they gathered in a great council, a kurultay, and declared: "We are many now, and we fear no enemy. We will meet our friends and wrestle with our foes!" But however hard they searched, they could find no way out of the mountains.

The iron mountain and the grey wolf

Then a blacksmith stepped forward and said: "There is iron in these mountains. If we melt the iron and open a road, perhaps the mountain will give way to us."

On his advice they stacked the foot of the mountain with alternating layers of wood and coal. They made seventy bellows from hides and fanned the fire for days, until the iron heart of the mountain glowed red, melted and opened a road wide enough for a fully loaded camel to pass through.

At that moment a Bozkurt, a grey wolf, appeared as if sent from the sky and stood in front of them. They understood that it had come to guide them. "The grey wolf will show us the way," they said, and followed it out of Ergenekon. In the best-known Turkish version, the khan who led them out was Börte Çine.

Return

Once outside, they forged weapons and tools from the mountain's iron and marched against the enemy who had nearly wiped them out. Many fell in the fighting, but after great hardship they won. They had their revenge on the Tatars and took back the lands their ancestors had lost.

The day they left Ergenekon was never forgotten. Every year it was celebrated as a festival: the khan would heat a piece of iron in the fire and strike it with a hammer on the anvil, and the leading men of the tribe would do the same after him, in memory of the mountain that had melted to set their people free.

Where the story comes from

Ergenekon has come down to us mainly through two chronicles. The older is Jami' al-Tawarikh, the great world history compiled by Rashid al-Din, a vizier of the Mongol Ilkhanate, in the early fourteenth century, where the refuge story is told about the ancestors of the Mongols. The second is Şecere-i Türk (The Genealogy of the Turks), written in the seventeenth century by Abu al-Ghazi Bahadur Khan, ruler of Khiva, which contains the fuller version with the blacksmith, the melted mountain and the festival. The wolf as a guide and ancestor is much older still: Chinese chronicles record the Göktürks' belief that they descended from a she-wolf.

For Turks, Ergenekon is above all a story of resilience and rebirth: a people brought to the edge of extinction, who survived in hiding, grew strong again and, with the guidance of the grey wolf and their own hands, forged their way back into the world.
