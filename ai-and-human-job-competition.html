Will Artificial Intelligence Replace My Job?

  • Baha Erdoğan
  • AI

Whenever I talk about AI with friends, the conversation drifts to the same place sooner or later. AI can do a huge range of things, and its effect on jobs is only one of the changes it will bring, yet this is the question I hear most, whether I'm part of the conversation or just overhearing one.

The topic turned into hype very quickly. I started working in AI engineering on international projects long before ChatGPT existed, and I try to stick to the view I had back then, without getting carried away by today's hype or today's backlash. That isn't always easy, but it's how I approach questions like this one. My method is simple: what do the economics and the technology actually allow? Maybe that's an occupational hazard of being an engineer.

For any job, two questions start running through my head:

  1. Is it feasible? This is also where my cynical break-even analysis comes in.
  2. What does it cost?

So instead of guessing, ask those two questions about your own work. They tell you a lot about whether AI is coming for it.

You're probably safe if:

  • Your job needs a lot of movement, even if not much physical strength.
  • It involves real representation: dealing with people face to face, speaking on behalf of others, being accountable in person.
  • You don't cost your employer much.
  • The work needs a lot of supervision and judgement on the spot.

You're at high risk if:

  • Most of your work is done sitting down and thinking, and you are not among the best in your field, or you are new to it.
  • You cost your employer a lot: salary, benefits, insurance, meals, transport, visas, training and so on.

The cost factor

In those high-risk cases, two things happen at once. The main costs of AI, hardware and adapting a system to a specific job, keep falling while its output keeps improving. Or your own cost is so high that building an AI system just for your job becomes a big win for your employer. Either way, change comes quickly.

What we see in the market supports this. Well-paid jobs that consist mostly of processing information are being reorganised under the label of AI "support". In finance, for example, a large part of routine analyst work, such as pulling data together, screening reports and drafting first versions of analyses, can already be automated.

As factory automation improves, more of the physical work will shift to robots too. Suppose the daily minimum wage is X, a collaborative robot costs X/50 a day to run, and its cost over its whole lifetime pays off. At that point the transformation speeds up.

China matters a great deal here. It is extremely good at driving costs down. Even if it doesn't use these machines to replace its own workers first, selling equipment to other countries that pays for itself very quickly could easily put it in the lead.

Who is safer in the short term?

In developing countries where labour is cheap, workers may not be in immediate danger. In the long run, though, automation is likely to pull production back to developed countries, and the workers in developing countries will lose their jobs anyway.

Think about it step by step:

  • Why did production move to developing countries in the first place? Because it was cheaper.
  • Where did that cost advantage come from? Low wages, weak safety standards, and governments that found it easier to tax workers than the companies employing them.
  • What happens now? The number of workers needed drops to a minimum, and high-quality production becomes cheaper than the cheapest human labour anywhere. Since robots are doing the work, safety rules stop being such a big cost either.

Developed countries, with their grip on natural resources and nuclear power, will have cheaper electricity. Add their established brands, and they get even stronger. The gap widens.

We are heading for a much more centralised world, where you will feel more and more that everything revolves around the most important neighbourhood of the biggest city of the biggest country, and where there are far fewer middlemen. In that world I don't believe tax systems can stay the way they are. If this part interests you, I recommend my article Why High Value-Per-Kilogram Exports Are the Key to National Prosperity.

All things considered

This is the part worth remembering. If your work produces things, writes code, performs surgery, cuts hair or treats teeth in a way that can't become part of a mass-production process, AI won't push you aside any time soon.

But if someone you trained for a while, explained the steps to, and who doesn't have your experience could do your job instead, and if that kind of work makes up most of what you do, you will most likely need to move into a different role.

Something that happened to me

Years ago, as part of a project in a European country, I visited a company that built automation equipment for factories. They told us proudly that they could run most of their own production without any people at all.

The presentation room had a large window looking down onto the factory floor. When I looked down, I saw that the entire production was being done by people. I still remember the silence in the room when I pointed at it during the presentation. That was when I started thinking about my "if your work can't become part of a mass-production process" theory.
