From Prediction to Impact: What Artificial Intelligence Actually Means for You

  • Baha Erdoğan
  • AI
You don't need to understand AI for it to affect you. Understanding it just helps you notice when it does.

Open a map app and the arrival time you see is a prediction. Scroll through a feed and the order of the posts is a prediction about what will hold your attention. Pay by card and, within a fraction of a second, a model has estimated how likely the payment is to be fraud. Most of the AI around you isn't a chatbot. It is a steady stream of small predictions made in the background, and together they decide a large part of how the digital world treats you.

From rules to predictions

Traditional software follows rules a programmer wrote down: if the balance is below zero, decline the payment. Machine learning replaces many of those hand-written rules with patterns learned from data. Nobody writes a rule describing what a fraudulent payment looks like. Instead, a model is shown millions of past payments, each marked as genuine or fraudulent, and works out the pattern itself.

The recipe is always roughly the same: collect data, find patterns in it, then use those patterns to make predictions about new cases in real time. Andrew Ng once called AI "the new electricity", and the comparison holds in at least one way. Like electricity, it mostly disappears into the infrastructure, and you only notice it when something goes wrong.

Where you already meet it

  • Health. Models help read medical images and flag risks early. In a 2018 study, DeepMind and Moorfields Eye Hospital presented a system that recommended the correct referral for more than 50 eye diseases about as accurately as leading specialists.
  • Banking. Fraud detection, credit scoring and automated investment services (robo-advisers) all run on models.
  • Shopping. Amazon's recommendations grew out of item-to-item collaborative filtering: people who bought this also bought that. On many sites, prices now move with predicted demand.
  • Social media and streaming. Your feed, your "For You" page and your Spotify playlists are ranked by models predicting what you will watch, like or play next.
  • Getting around. Navigation apps forecast traffic, ride-hailing apps predict demand and set prices, and driver-assistance systems recognise lanes and road signs.
  • Learning and writing. Writing assistants and language apps give instant feedback and adjust the next exercise to the mistakes you just made.
  • Logistics and farming. Carriers forecast demand and plan routes; farmers use drone images and models to spot crop disease before it spreads.

What it costs to put a model in an app

Using machine learning in everyday products is mostly a matter of trade-offs, and you feel them as a user even when you can't see them. The first is speed. A model running on your phone answers in a few milliseconds and your data never leaves the device, but the model has to be small. A large model in the cloud is far more capable, yet every request travels over the network, which adds a noticeable delay and means your data ends up on someone else's server.

Where the model runsTypical delayPrivacyMain limitation
On the device (Core ML, ONNX Runtime)Under 10 msData stays on the phoneMemory and battery
Nearby servers with optimised modelsTens of millisecondsData is sent over an encrypted connectionServer and GPU costs
Large language models in the cloudHalf a second to several secondsMore of your data leaves the deviceSlow, unpredictable responses at busy times

The second trade-off is accuracy against side effects:

  • Recommendation systems tuned for engagement keep showing you more of what is already popular, and niche content gets harder to find.
  • Fraud models that are too strict cut the bank's losses but block genuine customers at the till.
  • Autocomplete saves typing, but nudges everyone toward the same phrases and can repeat biases from its training data.

What this means for you

AI is no longer a distant or theoretical subject. It sits inside decisions about what you read, what you pay and sometimes whether you get a loan. You don't need to learn to code to deal with that, but a basic feel for how these systems work makes you a better-informed customer, employee and citizen.

  • Stay curious. Learn the basic ideas and vocabulary: training data, prediction, recommendation, bias. That is enough to follow most debates.
  • Ask why. When a recommendation appears, ask what it is optimised for. Is it helping you, or steering you?
  • Expect transparency. As models move into public services, hiring and lending, it is fair to ask how decisions are made and how you can challenge them.

The difference is who uses it

AI doesn't so much replace people as amplify whoever uses it well. Whether you run a business, study, make policy or make art, knowing roughly how these systems work and where they fail is becoming part of basic digital literacy, much as knowing how to search the web was twenty years ago.
