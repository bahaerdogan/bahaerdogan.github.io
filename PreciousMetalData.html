One of the Precious Metals: Data

  • Baha Erdoğan
  • Technology

Buy a television today and the specification sheet will tell you about the panel, the refresh rate and the processor. What it probably won't mention is that many smart TVs can recognise what is on the screen and report it back. In 2017 the US manufacturer Vizio agreed to pay 2.2 million dollars to settle charges that it had collected viewing data from millions of sets without their owners' consent. For a manufacturer, knowing what people watch, for how long and when they switch off can be worth more than the margin on the hardware.

The same trade sits underneath most of the technology we use. The phone, the car, the fitness watch and the smart speaker are all very good at what they do, and nearly all of them also produce a steady stream of data about the person using them. That stream is what this post is about.

Why data has become so valuable

1. Every piece of data records something that actually happened

A click, a purchase, the route you took home, a heartbeat measured at three in the morning: each is tied to a particular person, place and moment. You can't produce them again on demand, and you can't convincingly invent them at scale. That is where their value comes from. They describe what people actually did, not what they say they do or what a survey guessed.

2. AI is only as good as its data

Machine learning models learn entirely from examples. A model trained on poor, narrow or biased data makes poor, narrow or biased decisions, however clever the algorithm. With clean, varied and representative data, the same techniques help spot cancer earlier on scans, recommend music people actually enjoy and help cars avoid collisions. Data is what teaches a model what normal looks like and what should be flagged. No data, no learning; no learning, no "intelligence".

3. Data about people is the most valuable kind

Sensor readings from a machine are useful. Data about people, what they buy, where they go, what they search for late at night, is worth far more, because almost every business and public service ultimately exists to serve people. Collected with consent and handled carefully, it reveals health trends, uncovers unmet needs and makes services better. Handled carelessly, it can be used to manipulate the very people it describes.

More than the new oil

The phrase "data is the new oil" is usually credited to the British mathematician Clive Humby in 2006, and in 2017 The Economist argued that data had overtaken oil as the world's most valuable resource. The comparison works up to a point. Like crude oil, raw data is worth little until it has been refined, and it powers enormous industries.

The differences matter more, though. Oil is gone once you burn it; data can be used again and again, by many people at the same time. Oil reserves shrink, while data keeps growing every second. That is why I prefer the comparison in the title. Like gold or silver, data is something people dig for, refine and store, but unlike a metal it never wears out.

It has also become a kind of language. It is how your phone learns when to suggest the route to work, how a music app builds a playlist that suits your evening, how a sleep tracker notices that you sleep worse after a late coffee. And because it describes us, it is increasingly how we learn about ourselves, through patterns in our own behaviour we would never have spotted without it.

The ethical side

All of this raises questions that are not just technical. Who owns the data you produce every day: you, the device maker or the app? How long should it be kept, and who may it be sold to? Can you see what has been collected about you, and can you have it deleted?

Laws have started to answer some of these questions. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation, in force since 2018, gives people the right to see, correct and delete their personal data, and similar laws exist elsewhere, including Turkey's KVKK. But laws move slowly and technology moves fast, so a great deal still depends on how companies choose to behave and on how much attention users pay.

The aim should be a digital future that respects the people the data describes, and uses it to help them rather than to steer or exploit them.

A precious resource

It's easy to think of data as background noise, numbers floating around somewhere in the cloud. In reality it is the material today's digital life is built on, the link between human experience and what machines can understand. The technologies that will matter most in the coming years, from AI to smart cities, will depend on how much data we have, how good it is and how responsibly it is handled.

So the next time your phone unlocks because it recognises your face, or your assistant remembers your usual coffee order, remember that it isn't magic. It is data, one of the most precious materials of our time.
