Why Building an AI Startup Is So Hard: Production Reality

  • Baha Erdoğan
  • AI

Because I don't want you to get hurt…

AI products and AI startups are everywhere now. I'd even bet that you, reading this, have at least played with the idea of starting an AI company and striking it rich. For the past year I have been helping early-stage startups through consulting work, trying to get them over the line. The clearest thing I've seen from the inside is this: most of the problems we run into are no longer about being able to write code. They are about the engineering architecture behind the product and the operational reality of running it.

Because AI tools and code have become so easy to get hold of, many people see this as an effortless opportunity. Most founders I meet have already polished their idea with a large language model, talked it over with the people around them, and come to me with a plan they are convinced works on paper. That is not where the problems end. It is where they start.

1) No technical knowledge at all, and the "vibe coding" illusion

Many founders have started to underestimate software engineering and system architecture. A simple website and a pile of spaghetti code behind it, put together in a few hours with Claude or Cursor, keeps the founder's confidence sky-high, while the system itself can barely stay upright in production, even as a facade.

It honestly wears me out to explain why a prototype with no database schema, which locks up under concurrent load and has no API security or memory management to speak of, is not a "finished product". An interface that seems to work on localhost is not a scalable software architecture.

2) No real financial feasibility, and unit costs nobody calculated

The confidence from point one, the feeling of "I got an AI to build this, so I can do the rest myself", stops dead the moment an AI startup meets even its modest early running costs.

Tools and models change so fast that even in systems I design myself, I end up using completely different cloud platforms, vector databases and inference endpoints from one project to the next. There are times when even I struggle to work out where token costs, GPU rental and latency optimisation will end up once user traffic grows. If that takes careful work for an experienced engineer, a founder with no technical background has practically no chance of forecasting unit economics and cloud bills.

3) Weak teams and a geographic talent problem

Speaking about Turkey specifically, Istanbul has plenty of talented young engineers. But Istanbul combines the country's industrial base, its social life, its opportunities for growth, its financial centre and the heart of both its tech scene and its academia. That pulls good engineers there so strongly that founders in other cities are often left in the hands of people who aren't up to the job.

In projects in Anatolia, I keep meeting developers who have no real academic or technical foundation, and, worse, who regularly lie to founders to hide what they can't do. As a result, the systems built outside our largest cities are, in terms of architecture and code quality, frankly mostly garbage. There's a Turkish saying: "Koyunun bulunmadığı yerde keçiye Abdurrahman Çelebi derler", roughly "where there are no sheep, the goat gets called a gentleman", or as the English saying goes, in the land of the blind the one-eyed man is king. Unfortunately, we live the literal version of that saying in this industry.

4) The quick-money mindset and no sustainable budget

A separate problem from point two: even founders who actually have capital often haven't understood that a technology startup is not like running a shop, and that the return on investment can take a long time. In a traditional trade business, a few unpaid invoices can be made up for in the short term. In a tech startup, the same cash shortfall means critical infrastructure doesn't get built, and that means market opportunities get missed.

When I tell founders they may have to spend money for a whole year on fine-tuning, data cleaning, servers and R&D without earning a single lira, the initial excitement turns into panic.

5) The engineering order, turned upside down

If I lay out the right order for building a product as an engineer, it looks like this:

  1. First, and easiest on paper but most critical, sketch out the idea: the data flow and the system architecture.
  2. Second, the paperwork: regulations, store approvals, API access.
  3. Third and last, actually build it: the code, the backend, the model layers, and putting all the technical pieces together.

What founders actually do is this: give Claude a prompt and get a draft (and assume it is a perfect, finished product), immediately start fighting with store approvals and deployment, and when the system blows up at the first real integration, panic and call an engineer. Notice that the foundation, sketching the idea and designing the architecture, was skipped completely.

6) Not realising how big the competition is

This one mostly explains itself, but here is how it looks in practice. Founders who have only just started hook an open-source model or a ready-made API up to their own interface (an "AI wrapper") and genuinely believe they can compete with global tech giants that have billions of dollars of GPU clusters, their own data pipelines and research teams of hundreds of people. They don't factor in that a big model provider like OpenAI, Anthropic or Google can ship the exact thing their startup does as a free, standard feature in a single update.

7) Not being able to tell a valuable idea from a worthless one

Without a grounding in computer science, algorithms and data structures, a founder can't tell which problem really needs deep learning or machine learning and which can be solved with a few if-else rules or an ordinary database query. With no original dataset, no domain expertise and no well-built RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) architecture behind them, shallow ideas that amount to "ChatGPT bolted onto a form" get pitched as revolutionary inventions. Not being able to tell real engineering value from garbage burns through both time and money.

Conclusion: writing prompts is not building systems

There's no question that AI tools have made writing code faster and lowered the bar for building prototypes. But there is a huge engineering gap between getting a working demo out of a language model and building an AI product that thousands of people use at the same time, that answers within milliseconds, that keeps data safe and that pays for itself.

Most AI startups that shut down today don't fail because "the AI didn't work". They fail because they threw away the basics of software engineering, system architecture, unit cost calculation and building the right team, on the assumption that "we don't need those any more". If you really want to build a technology company that lasts, don't start by having a language model write your code. Start by sketching out the problem you're solving, calculating the costs down to the last cent, and sitting down with engineers who actually know how to build systems.
