Why High Value-Per-Kilogram Exports Are the Key to National Prosperity

  • Baha Erdoğan
  • Finance

In recent years I've become a little obsessed with one number: export revenue per kilogram. Not just as an economic statistic, but as a deeper indicator of how well a country is doing. It isn't only about putting more money in the pockets of workers and business owners. When a country raises the unit value of what it exports, by making and selling goods with more value added, the effects spread through its economy, its society and even its environment.

Let me explain with a deliberately ordinary example: cheese.

What you really sell when you sell cheese

Suppose you make cheese. If it is exceptionally good and carries a recognised brand, buyers at home and abroad will happily pay a premium for it. Over time, what you sell stops being "just cheese". It becomes part of a wider experience that includes taste, quality, prestige and even a little image.

One of the most interesting things about high value-added production is that you can sell the identity that comes with consuming the product. People who buy your cheese don't just enjoy something delicious. When they serve it to guests, it says something about their taste and that they can be trusted to choose well. That intangible value becomes part of the decision to buy, often without the buyer noticing. They are paying for the product, and also for the experience and the impression it creates.

It goes further than taste. The value extends to the seller's brand, the look of the packaging, even the small satisfaction of carrying it home in a bag that passers-by might notice. If that sounds abstract, pay attention to your own feelings next time you go shopping. You'll see how much of what we buy is really an experience, and when that experience is well designed and satisfying, people pay more for it.

What happens when value per kilogram rises across a whole sector

Now go back to how that premium cheese is made. Good cheese starts with good milk, so dairy farmers have to look after their animals better: regular visits from the vet, clean and humane conditions, healthier feed. Healthier animals mean fewer diseases, including some that can pass to people. A healthier farm protects the farmer's family and the wider community. Fewer sick days mean families do better and children miss less school, which shows up in how they do later on.

Cleaner farms also smell less, which makes the whole village a more pleasant place to live. Higher demand for good milk pushes farmers to buy better feed, which in turn lifts crop farming: growers invest in better seed, use fewer pesticides and work more closely with agronomists. Soil gets healthier, and better food reaches not only the animals but people too, children above all. The long-run result is healthier generations.

With slightly better margins, farmers can build safer homes and leave their children solid property instead of a mortgage. That frees up income later for hobbies, education or starting a business, and over time it builds a happier and more resilient society.

This may sound like a chain of metaphors. It isn't. It is how the real economy behaves when the focus shifts from volume to value.

The trap of cheap, high-volume exports

It is becoming increasingly clear that national prosperity can't be built on exporting large volumes of cheap goods. That strategy can push up nominal GDP and create an impression of economic power, but it often hides structural weaknesses and real social costs.

India is a good example. It is often described as a rising economic superpower, and by the IMF's estimates it overtook Japan in 2025 to become the world's fourth-largest economy. That ranking alone is misleading, though. Measured by export value per kilogram, a rough proxy for technological sophistication, India is far behind countries of similar economic size. Its exports lean heavily on raw materials, textiles and basic manufacturing, with comparatively little added value. Japan, by contrast, exports high-tech, high-margin products and has one of the highest export values per kilogram in the world.

The difference has wide consequences. Low-value exports hold wages down and lock in a dependence on cheap labour. They come with low productivity, environmental damage and little investment in research or in workers' welfare. Products from low-cost economies often fail to meet the quality standards of rich markets, which makes them less competitive there, while people at home make do with lower-quality goods in areas as basic as food, healthcare and infrastructure.

This shows up directly in people's lives. India's GDP per person was around $2,600 in 2023, Japan's around $34,000, more than twelve times as much. Life expectancy is roughly 70 years in India and about 84 in Japan, and the gap in infant mortality, education and public health is just as stark. Weaknesses like these deepen inequality, block social mobility and feed discontent.

Worse, a country that gets locked into a low-value export model risks drifting into something close to modern economic servitude: people stuck with long hours, insecure jobs and poor public services, all so that cheap goods can keep flowing to richer countries. That isn't sustainable growth. It is collapse postponed.

Research supports this view. Philipp Koch and Clemens Fessler tested the Heckscher-Ohlin model, one of the classic theories of international trade, using data on the value added in exports. They found that countries with abundant labour tend to export value added in labour-intensive industries, which suggests that labour-rich countries like India naturally drift toward labour-intensive, low value-added exports unless they deliberately steer away from them.

The road to real prosperity runs toward high value-added sectors, the ones that earn the most per unit of resource rather than just per head. By raising export value per kilogram through innovation, design, branding and quality, countries can escape the false promise of scale and move toward genuine human development.

Türkiye's window of opportunity

I believe we are entering a golden period, full of opportunity but short. In that window, countries like Türkiye need to move decisively toward high value-added sectors, especially information technology, advanced manufacturing, biotechnology and green energy.

There is real progress, which is encouraging. It isn't enough yet. We need to double down on policies that move us from competing on cost to competing on value.

Technology is our modern gold mine. Instead of digging deeper into cheap labour and quick exports, we should put our collective energy into R&D, design, branding and digital infrastructure. That is how lasting wealth is built, not only for exporters but for everyone along the value chain.

Raising the value of every kilogram we export doesn't just mean selling better products. It means building better lives.
